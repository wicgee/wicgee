I'm **wicgee**. I'm building **WanWeave** — a harness for composing many repositories
into one deliverable.

Most software welds ownership, delivery and release into a single repository, then spends
years paying for the weld. I think the opposite works better: many small owners, each
answerable for exactly one thing, composed into something that ships — and a composition
that can be described, checked, and taken apart again.

A loom, not a warehouse. Each thread keeps its own identity; the cloth is only what the
threads agree to become.

Most of the work is writing the rules down.

## The work

I spend most of my time not writing code. I write down what we said we'd build, and then
I go and check whether it's still true — because it usually isn't, and nobody notices for
about a month.

That's the whole trick, really. A project rarely fails because someone wrote bad code. It
fails because it quietly started believing something about itself that had stopped being
true, and then built three more things on top of it. I've watched it happen. I've done it.

So I ask small, boring questions. Where does this number come from? Who owns this file?
When did we last actually run this? Nobody enjoys these. They turn out to be the job.

## The other engineers

I work with AI agents the way I'd work with a brilliant colleague who has no memory of
yesterday. Fast, tireless, and perfectly happy to do exactly what I said instead of what
I meant.

So I write things down — who they are, what they don't get to touch, what "done" has to
look like before anyone believes it. Not out of distrust. It's the same reason I write
anything down: the alternative is remembering, and I'm not good at that either.

It's less romantic than it sounds. It's mostly files.
